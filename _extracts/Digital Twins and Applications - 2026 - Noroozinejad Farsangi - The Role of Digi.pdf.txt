ABSTRACT
Digital twin technology, continuously updated, high‐fidelity virtual replicas of physical assets, has matured rapidly in aerospace 
and is now poised to transform construction in extreme environments on Earth and beyond. This Opinion Article synthesises 
recent research, global case studies, and policy developments to argue that digital twins are pivotal for planning, autonomous 
execution, and predictive infrastructure maintenance on the Moon, Mars, the deep ocean, polar regions, and remote deserts. By 
coupling real‐time sensor data with physics‐based and AI‐driven simulation, twins enable risk‐free prototyping, swarm‐robot 
coordination, and lifecycle resilience while reducing environmental footprints. We highlight strategic implications, data‐ 
governance frameworks, workforce upskilling, and federated twin ecosystems and forecast emerging trends such as AI‐ 
enhanced autonomy, XR interfaces, and smart‐material feedback loops. The article aims to inform multidisciplinary re-
searchers, engineers, and policymakers by advocating the early adoption of digital twin standards to accelerate sustainable 
resilient construction in the most challenging frontiers.
1|Introduction
Extreme environments, from the vacuum of space to the depths 
of the ocean and the frozen expanse of the Arctic, pose un-
precedented challenges to construction and infrastructure 
development. Building and maintaining structures in these 
settings is fraught with difficulties: human access is limited or 
dangerous, conditions are unpredictable, real‐time communi -
cation may be delayed, and mistakes or failures can be costly or 
life‐threatening. As humanity prepares to establish a sustained 
presence on the Moon and Mars and expands operations in 
remote terrestrial regions, there is a pressing need for technol -
ogies to mitigate these challenges. Digital twin technology has 
rapidly gained attention as a potential game‐changer in this 
context. A digital twin is essentially a living digital replica of a 
physical asset or system, kept continuously in sync with the real 
world via sensors, data streams, and simulations. By providing 
an up‐to‐date virtual mirror of physical reality, digital twins enable engineers and operators to monitor conditions in inac -
cessible locations, predict issues before they occur, and even 
rehearse complex operations in silico, all of which are invalu -
able for extreme environment construction [1–10 ].
The concept of the digital twin originated in aerospace and 
defence contexts and is deeply rooted in NASA's early experi -
ences. During the Apollo programme of the 1960s–70s, NASA 
developed what could be viewed as the first digital twin: a ‘living 
model’ of the Apollo spacecraft that mission control used to di-
agnose and solve problems remotely. In the Apollo 13 crisis of 
1970, for example, simulators and physical models augmented 
with real telemetry were used to replicate the damaged space -
craft's condition, allowing engineers on Earth to devise a suc-
cessful rescue procedure. This paved the way for the formal 
concept, decades later, NASA's John Vickers coined the term 
‘digital twin ’ around 2010 as the agency embraced model‐based 
engineering for next‐generation programs. Since then, digital

intervening or re‐planning tasks through that virtual interface.

alerting operators to emerging risks such as permafrost thaw or 
material fatigue long before a failure occurs [1, 16–19 ].

sustainability but also require policy support in terms of 
standards, investment, and collaboration frameworks.
•Finally, we look ahead to future trends, including integra -
tion with AI and machine learning, robotics and autono -
mous swarms, extended reality interfaces, smart materials, 
and interconnected digital twin ecosystems poised to shape 
the next generation of construction technology.

1.1|Definition and Foundational Principles of 
Digital Twins
A digital twin can be defined as a virtual representation of a 
physical object, system, or process that is updated continuously 
with real‐world data and used to inform decisions through 
simulation and analysis. In essence, it is a computerised clone of 
a real entity, mirroring that entity's state and behaviour in near 
real‐time. Several authoritative definitions converge on the key 
characteristics of digital twins. For example, a 2024 National 
Academies report defines a digital twin as ‘a set of virtual in-
formation constructs that mimics the structure, context, and 
behaviour of a natural or engineered system… is dynamically 
updated with data from its physical twin, has predictive capa -
bility, and informs decisions that realise value, ’ emphasising that 
bidirectional interaction between the physical and virtual sys-
tem is central. The Digital Twin Consortium similarly describes 
it as ‘an integrated data‐driven virtual representation of real‐ 
world entities and processes, with synchronised interaction at a 
specified frequency and fidelity, ’ highlighting the continuous 
synchronisation of data. In simpler terms, the digital twin stays 
in lockstep with its physical counterpart: as the physical asset 
changes or operates, sensors and data feeds update the digital 
model; conversely, analyses or control actions from the digital 
side can be applied back to the physical side [13, 20–22 ].
Foundational principles that underpin digital twins are depicted 
in Figure 2.
•Real‐Time Data Synchronisation: Digital twins rely on 
data streams from sensors (e.g. IoT devices embedded in 
equipment, structural health monitors, environmental 
sensors) to ensure the virtual model reflects current reality. 
This could be high‐frequency telemetry from a machine or 
periodic updates on ecological conditions. The constant 
data flow enables the twin to be a live mirror of the system's 
state [23–25 ].
•Simulation and Predictive Modelling: A digital twin is 
not a static model; it is usually coupled with physics‐based 
models, simulations, or AI algorithms that can predict 
future behaviour or test scenarios. By leveraging high‐ 
performance computing, digital twins can answer ‘what 
if’ questions, such as how a lunar habitat will respond to a 
meteoroid strike or how an underwater pipeline would 
behave in a severe storm without risking the real asset. This 
predictive capability allows proactive interventions (main -
tenance, design changes, contingency planning) before real 
problems occur [26–30 ].
•Bidirectional Feedback and Control: Importantly, digi-
tal twins are connected to their physical twins in a feedback 
loop. Not only does data flow from the physical to the 
digital (to update the model) but insights and commands 
can flow from digital to physical. In practice, this means an operator could use the digital twin to send optimised con-
trol inputs to a robot or adjust the operating parameters of 
remote equipment. For example, if a digital twin detects via 
simulation that a lunar rover's motor is overheating, it 
might trigger a command to reduce speed or suggest a 
cooling pause, thus directly influencing the physical oper -
ation [25, 31, 32].
•Lifecycle Span: A true digital twin often spans the entire 
lifecycle of an asset from design and construction through 
operations and maintenance to eventual decommissioning. 
In the design phase, the twin (sometimes called a virtual 
prototype) allows testing and optimisation. During con-
struction, it can track progress and quality. In operations, it 
monitors performance and degradation. And at end‐of‐life, 
it can assist in safely decommissioning or repurposing as-
sets. This lifecycle perspective is crucial for long‐term 
infrastructure such as space habitats or remote in-
stallations, ensuring continuity of knowledge and data [16, 
26, 27, 32].
•High Fidelity and Integration of Heterogeneous Data: 
Digital twins integrate data from multiple sources and 
disciplines, such as structural, mechanical, electrical, 
environmental, and even human factors data. The fidelity 
can range from coarse (for strategic planning) to ultra‐fine 
(for detailed engineering). For instance, a digital twin of a 
spacecraft might encompass its 3D CAD geometry, sensor 
readings from its subsystems, orbital dynamics models, and 
even software states. The richer and more accurate this 
integrated model, the more value it provides. In construc -
tion, this means merging BIM data, schedule (4D) data, cost 
(5D) data, and real‐time sensor data into one cohesive 
virtual space [16, 21, 28, 33].
Together, these principles allow digital twins to function as 
‘living’ models of their physical counterparts, supporting what 
NASA researchers have called a shift towards ‘living models ’ or 
‘living digital simulations ’ that evolve alongside the system [34]. 
The idea of continuously living models marks a departure from 
older practice where simulations were one‐off or sporadic. 
Instead, the digital twin is constantly running and continuously 
updated, providing an ongoing decision‐support tool.
It is crucial to differentiate digital twins from related concepts. A 
digital twin is more than a simple CAD model or a static 3D 
visualisation; it is also more than just raw sensor data. Instead, it 
is the fusion of model +data +analytics in a closed loop. In the 
construction context, one can think of a digital twin as BIM plus 
live data plus intelligence [35]. Similarly, terms such as digital 
shadow or digital model sometimes refer to subsets of this idea. 
A digital shadow might imply a one‐way data flow (physical to 
digital only), lacking interactive, predictive elements. A fully 
realised digital twin, by contrast, has two‐way interactivity and 
autonomous updating.
From a systems engineering perspective, digital twins are an 
implementation of cyber‐physical systems. They tie together the 
physical domain and the cyber domain, requiring robust system 
integration. Key enabling technologies include IoT sensor net-
works (to gather data), cloud and edge computing (to process 
data and host models), and connectivity (to transmit data,

A significant technical challenge for digital twin systems in 
extreme environments is data synchronisation under con-
strained connectivity, such as deep space, polar regions, or 
remote terrestrial sites with limited bandwidth or intermittent 
links. Traditional real‐time streaming of high‐frequency sensor 
data is often infeasible in such contexts. To address this, systems 
adopt edge computing and model‐informed buffering ap-
proaches: raw sensor data are pre‐processed locally to extract 
essential features and anomalies, and only compressed or high‐ 
value summaries are transmitted to the remote twin, whereas 
less critical data are stored and forwarded when connectivity 
permits. This reduces bandwidth demand while preserving the 
twin's operational fidelity. Concurrently, predictive models, 
including physics‐based simulations and machine learning es-
timators, fill in gaps during communication blackouts, allowing 
the twin to maintain an up‐to‐date representation based on the 
last known state and expected dynamics. Literature indicates 
that this hybrid approach, combining local processing, priori -
tised transmission, and predictive interpolation, enables digital 
twins to function effectively despite low and variable bandwidth 
conditions [36, 37]. Additionally, federated and distributed ar-
chitectures leverage edge AI and sensor fusion to support syn-
chronisation in bandwidth‐limited scenarios, which is also a 
trending research direction [38].
To summarise, a digital twin is the convergence of a virtual 
model with the real world in real‐time, aimed at improving 
understanding and decision‐making. This foundational concept 
will be referenced throughout our discussion of how digital 
twins can assist space and remote construction. By having a firm grasp of what digital twins are and what they can do, we can 
better evaluate their current use and untapped potential in the 
following sections.
1.2|Current State and Technological Maturity in 
Construction and Space Sectors
Digital twin technology has reached markedly different levels 
of maturity in the space sector (and related aerospace in-
dustry) versus the construction sector. Understanding this 
disparity provides context for how much work remains to 
fully leverage digital twins in construction, especially for 
extreme environments.
Space Sector Maturity: The aerospace and space exploration 
community is widely regarded as an early adopter and leader in 
digital twin development. NASA and major aerospace com -
panies have used high‐fidelity simulations and digital replicas 
for decades as part of design, testing, and operations. In fact, 
aerospace was ‘the first industry to disseminate the concept and 
use of DTs, ’ starting around 2010 when NASA formally inte-
grated digital twins into its technology roadmaps [2–4]. Since 
then, the sector has produced a number of prominent digital 
twin applications:
•Spacecraft and Aircraft Systems: Companies like Boeing 
and Airbus employ digital twins to monitor the health of 
aircraft in operation and improve designs. A well‐cited 
example is Boeing's use of a digital twin for the 787 
Dreamliner to analyse its electrical battery systems after 
service incidents. In the space realm, NASA created digital 
twins for the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) during 
its development. Because JWST's huge folded structure 
couldn't be fully physically tested on Earth, NASA built a 
digital twin to simulate its thermal behaviour in orbit and

•On‐Orbit Operations and Satellites: Satellite operators 
use digital twins to manage constellations and predict 
orbital decay or collision risks. As an illustration, SpaceX 
utilises digital twin technology to optimise the design and 
testing of its Starship, simulating the entire lifecycle of the 
vehicle under extreme conditions to identify issues before 
actual flights. The Starship programme's iterative rapid 
prototyping approach is underpinned by intensive simula -
tion (essentially treating each prototype and launch as 
feeding data back into a refined digital model). Even for 
astronaut training, mixed‐reality simulations incorporate 
digital twin elements: one training platform, drawing on 
NASA and ESA systems, virtually replicates the ISS (In-
ternational Space Station) and SpaceX's Falcon Heavy 
rocket so that astronauts can practice missions and main -
tenance tasks in a realistic digital environment.
•Planetary Rovers and Landers: NASA's Mars rover teams 
have long used a sort of digital twin for operations. For 
example, the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers on Mars 
each have an Earth‐based replica and a physics‐based 
simulator that is fed telemetry from Mars, enabling oper -
ators to try out manoeuvres virtually before commanding 
the real rover. This reduces risk when driving over un-
known terrain or using the robotic arm. The concept is now 
extending to the Moon: as part of the Artemis programme, 
NASA's Lunar Surface Innovation Initiative has developed 
a digital twin of a proposed regolith excavator robot (the 
ISRU Pilot Excavator, IPEx). Student teams in a recent 
NASA challenge had to write software agents to navigate 
this digital twin of the lunar excavator in a simulated moon 
terrain, demonstrating how future lunar robots will likely 
be managed via their digital counterparts.
Overall, the space sector has achieved a high technological 
readiness for digital twins in specific domains (design/testing of 
vehicles, spacecraft operations). Many space digital twins are at 
Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 7–9 (demonstrated in the 
operational environment or actual use) for specific applications 
such as spacecraft health management. Agencies such as NASA 
and ESA continue to invest heavily: NASA's 2022 Digital Twin 
Strategic Framework explicitly identifies digital twins as a key 
‘technology foundation’ for enabling complex missions and 
autonomy. There are, however, still challenges specific to space. 
For instance, dealing with limited bandwidth and intermittent 
connectivity (a Mars habitat twin must sync data over a 20‐min 
one‐way delay), and ensuring models remain accurate under the 
extreme physics of space (microgravity, radiation effects, etc.). 
But directionally, the trend is clear: as missions become more 
autonomous and farther from Earth, digital twins become 
indispensable for mission control and for the assets themselves 
to manage uncertainty.Construction Sector Maturity: By contrast, the architecture, 
engineering and construction (AEC) industry has been slower in 
adopting digital twins and is considered to be in the early stages 
of maturity. The concept of digital twins entered construction 
discussions more recently, piggybacking on the spread of BIM 
and smart infrastructure initiatives [1, 16, 17]. Several factors 
contribute to this lag:
•The construction industry is traditionally fragmented and 
project‐based, which makes standardisation of digital twin 
approaches difficult. Early adopters exist, but there is not 
yet a widely adopted standard or framework for what a 
construction digital twin should include, unlike aerospace, 
where NASA and others set clear digital twin definitions 
early on. The terminology can be inconsistent (some firms 
label enhanced 3D models as ‘digital twins’ even if they 
lack live data links).
•Most current applications in construction are limited in 
scope. Digital twins in building operations (sometimes 
called ‘smart building’ twins or city twins) are being used to 
monitor energy usage, HVAC performance, or space uti-
lisation in real‐time. For example, a commercial building 
might have a digital twin that integrates its BIM model with 
IoT sensors for temperature and occupancy, helping facil -
ities managers optimise HVAC settings to save energy. 
Similarly, infrastructure operators (such as bridge or rail 
owners) have begun to create digital twins of assets to track 
structural health. For example, the UK has piloted digital 
twins for bridges where sensor data on strain and vibration 
feeds into a model that predicts maintenance needs.
•There are notable pilot implementations. Singapore's ‘Vir-
tual Singapore’ is a digital twin of the entire city to aid urban 
planning. Various large construction/engineering firms have 
been experimenting; for instance, Kiewit and Fluor have 
used digital models linked with construction site sensors 
(drones doing reality capture to update progress in the 
model). In one case, a Chinese project created a digital twin 
of a new high‐rise during construction to track deviations 
and prevent schedule delays, essentially a 4D (3D +time) 
twin that updated daily with actual site data. These efforts 
show promise but are not yet routine on all projects.
•Industry analyses show rapidly growing interest. The global 
market for digital twins in construction is expanding at 
~30%+CAGR, projected to reach tens of billions USD by 
the end of the decade. This growth is fuelled by the inter -
section of BIM, IoT, and project management tools. How -
ever, moving from interest to full deployment is slow due to 
cost and skill barriers.
Despite being behind aerospace, the trajectory is positive. Many 
construction firms now recognise digital twins as key to digital 
transformation in construction (often termed ‘Construction 
4.0’). A recent survey indicated a sharp rise (~40x increase from 
2010s to early 2020s) in research publications linking ‘digital 
twin’ with construction and maintenance topics, reflecting 
burgeoning R&D. Industry consortia (such as the Digital Twin 
Consortium, which includes some AEC companies) and stan -
dards bodies are working on reference architectures specifically 
for construction and infrastructure digital twins.

2|Applications of Digital Twins in Space 
Construction
Space construction refers to the building of structures and 
infrastructure beyond Earth, including on the lunar or Martian 
surface, in orbit (orbital platforms, space stations, large tele-
scopes), or even in transit (spacecraft assembly). These activities 
are inherently remote (far from human operators) and occur in 
harsh conditions that preclude the constant presence of 
workers. Digital twins are poised to play a pivotal role in all 
phases of space construction by enabling comprehensive virtual 
prototyping, remote operations, and predictive maintenance 
capabilities. Below, we explore key application areas and pro-
vide examples (Figure 3):2.1|Virtual Prototyping and Design of Space 
Habitats and Structures
Before committing to physical construction on the Moon or 
Mars, engineers can build a complete digital twin of the pro-
posed structure or habitat. This goes beyond static CAD models; 
the twin can incorporate local environmental conditions 
(e.g., lunar gravity, radiation, regolith properties) and simulate 
the construction process. For instance, NASA and its partners 
have developed detailed simulations of a lunar base to test 
building methods such as 3D printing with regolith. A Chinese 
research team at HUST's National Digital Construction Tech -
nology Center created a ‘laboratory sandbox’ that simulates a 
future lunar base, where they experimented with various habitat 
designs (dome structures, egg‐shaped modules) under virtual 
lunar conditions. This digital sandbox allowed them to evaluate 
how different shapes would withstand the Moon's low gravity, 
temperature extremes (~300°C swings), moonquakes, and ra-
diation, leading them to an optimised ‘lunar pot’ double‐dome 
concept. Such virtual prototyping, essentially a digital twin of 
a not‐yet‐built habitat, is invaluable. It lets architects identify 
optimal designs that balance structural strength, material use, 
and habitability without costly real experiments on the Moon. 
Moreover, using physics‐based twins, one can simulate assem -
bly methods, for example, programme a swarm of construction 
robots in the digital environment to see if they can autono -
mously build the structure piece by piece (this ties into swarm 
robotics, discussed later) [39–41 ].

machine behaviour in a virtual environment. Their goal is that 
an excavator on the Moon can be controlled ‘without being 
anywhere near it’ by using the digital twin as the control

and the status of its systems, allowing operators on Earth to 
drive it as if playing a realistic video game—thus overcoming 
the distance and communication lag. This concept was proven 
on Earth in analogous scenarios: Hitachi in 2024 reported a 3D 
digital twin system for remote construction machines on Earth, 
where sensor‐laden bulldozers and cranes were controlled from 
afar with an interactive twin providing an ‘intuitive sense’ of 
location and movement. The result was a safer and easier 
remote operation that eliminated the need for expert skills, 
since the operator could essentially see a live virtual replica of 
the site and equipment. Translating this to the Moon, digital 
twins can allow relatively unskilled operators (or AI autopilots) 
to coordinate multiple robots digging regolith, assembling 
habitat modules, laying cables, etc., all via a synchronised vir-
tual site model. NASA's use of the IPEx excavator's twin in 
student competitions hints at this future, the students' autono -
mous software had to navigate the digital twin of the robot, 
essentially treating it as the real thing [42–44 ].
2.3|Construction Planning, Logistics, and 
Simulation
Space construction projects require meticulously choreographed 
logistics; every material is precious, and every error is magnified 
by distance. Digital twins of the construction process can 
simulate the entire workflow. For example, consider building a 
lunar landing pad from lunar concrete: a digital twin could 
simulate bulldozers collecting regolith, a regolith processing 
plant making bricks, robots laying the bricks, and so forth. 
Planners can vary parameters in the twin (number of robots, 
task sequences, energy availability, etc.) to optimise the plan 
prior to execution. This was illustrated by the European Space 
Agency (ESA) in analogue tests for a lunar base construction: 
ESA created virtual construction sites to test how autonomous 
robots might work together to build a lunar habitat, identifying 
bottlenecks such as one robot waiting on another and then 
tweaking the plan digitally. In one scenario, a swarm of small 
robots was simulated to coordinate similar to ants (inspired by 
ant colony behaviours) to excavate regolith and build a berm for 
radiation shielding. The digital twin environment allowed the 
testing of different swarm algorithms and communication 
strategies without risking hardware. In NASA's case, they have 
considered the in‐space assembly of large structures (like 
assembling a space telescope in orbit using robotic arms); here 
again, a digital twin of the structure and robots can run through the entire assembly sequence many times to iron out issues, 
much like how aerospace uses ‘digital rehearsals’ for complex 
deployments (as with JWST's sunshield deployment twin) 
[45–47 ].
2.4|Monitoring and Predictive Maintenance of 
Space Infrastructure
Once a structure or system is built in space, its digital twin 
becomes the central tool for monitoring health and scheduling 
maintenance. Space habitats or vehicles endure harsh condi -
tions (thermal cycling, radiation, micrometeoroids) and must 
often operate autonomously for long periods. A digital twin can 
continuously ingest data from structural sensors (strain gauges, 
temperature sensors, leak detectors, etc.) on, say, a lunar habitat 
and compare those readings against expected values from the 
twin's models. If the pressure in a section is dropping or a vi-
bration signature changes, the twin (via algorithms) might 
detect a probable leak or fatigue crack and alert operators before 
a catastrophic failure. This approach is akin to how modern jet 
engines on Earth are monitored by digital twins for early signs 
of trouble. On the ISS, a rudimentary form of this exists where 
ground control runs simulations using live telemetry to predict 
when a system will break and needs replacing. Digital twins 
would enhance that by being higher fidelity and possibly on‐site 
(running on the habitat's computer for immediate responses). 
An example in development is NASA's Gateway (the planned 
lunar orbit station); engineers are creating detailed digital 
models of Gateway's modules with the intention of using them 
for operations and troubleshooting once Gateway is in orbit, 
effectively having a virtual version of the station on the ground 
that mirrors the real one in space. Another example is the 
resilience of large space structures: as mentioned, JWST had a 
digital twin to monitor its mirror temps; future mega‐structures 
(such as a Mars transit ship or a lunar base's power system) will 
likely have persistent twins for continuous diagnostics. The 
predictive maintenance benefit is crucial when sending repair 
parts or crews is expensive and delayed—the twin can forecast 
when a component (e.g., a solar panel or life support filter) will 
degrade so that spares and repair missions can be planned well 
in advance [3, 15].
2.5|Operational Training and Simulation (XR for 
Construction Tasks)
Digital twins can be combined with extended reality (XR), 
including virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) to 
train astronauts and robotic operators for construction tasks. 
Before attempting a construction task on the Moon, the crew or 
robotics team on Earth can practice it in VR using the digital 
twin model of the site and the tools. NASA has embraced this 
concept: one training simulation uses digital twins of spacecraft 
subsystems and realistic physics in an immersive VR setup to 
train astronauts on launch procedures and EVAs (spacewalks). 
For construction, imagine an astronaut training to deploy a fold‐ 
out habitat or to operate a drilling rig on Mars. They would wear 
a VR headset that places them in a high‐fidelity digital twin of 
the environment, complete with correct terrain and equipment

construction activities. They allow engineers to virtually be on 
the Moon or Mars, designing, simulating, monitoring, and

because it enables precise recreation of site conditions and 
machine behaviour in the digital realm. The next section will 
see that many of these same capabilities, remote monitoring, 
simulation, and predictive analytics, are equally game‐changing 
for construction in Earth's remotest corners.
3|Applications of Digital Twins in Remote 
Terrestrial Construction
Remote terrestrial construction refers to building or managing 
infrastructure in locations on Earth that are extremely difficult 
to access regularly by people. This includes polar regions (Arctic 
and Antarctic sites), undersea environments, deserts and other 
isolated landscapes, and high‐risk zones. Such projects face 
some analogous challenges to space construction: limited on‐ 
site personnel, harsh weather or environmental conditions, logistical complications, and high stakes for failure. Digital 
twins can be leveraged in these contexts to improve planning, 
enable remote supervision, and ensure resilience (Figure 4).
We will discuss a few representative environments and how 
digital twins are applied:
3.1|Arctic and Antarctic Infrastructure
The far north and south of our planet host critical infrastructure 
such as research stations, roads, pipelines, and mines, all under 
severe cold and inaccessibility. Climate change is further 
destabilising these environments (e.g., thawing permafrost 
undermining foundations). Digital twins are emerging as vital 
tools to monitor these assets in real‐time and predict issues 
caused by environmental changes. For example, in Northern 
Canada and Alaska, many buildings and pipelines are built on 
permafrost. Engineers now use digital twins of these structures 
to track ground temperature, soil movements, and structural 
responses continuously. One policy report noted that ‘among the 
most promising tools is Digital Twin technology, which offers an 
evolving picture of how an asset is responding to environmental 
conditions… for infrastructure built on permafrost, this means 
engineers can track changes in ground temperature and moisture 
over time and predict how these changes might impact the stability 
of a road, pipeline, or building ’. Concretely, this might involve 
sensors in the ground measuring permafrost thaw depth and in 
the structure measuring tilt or strain; the twin assimilates these 
data and runs thermal‐structural models to forecast when the 
foundation will reach a critical level of settlement. Early 
warnings from the twin can trigger interventions (such as 
installing ground chillers or shoring up supports) to prevent 
catastrophic failures due to permafrost collapse.

3.2|Underwater Construction and Subsea 
Infrastructure
The ocean environment presents continuous challenges for the 
construction of oil rigs, subsea pipelines, wind turbines, and un-
derwater tunnels. These assets are submerged, not easily reach -
able for inspection, and subject to corrosion and pressure stresses. 
Digital twins have become integral in the subsea industry for 
maintenance and monitoring. A notable use case is the moni -
toring of offshore oil and gas infrastructure. Traditionally, com -
panies send divers or ROVs (remotely operated vehicles) every 
few years to inspect platforms and pipelines. Now, with digital 
twins, they can drastically reduce physical inspections. For 
example, the National Subsea Centre in the UK highlighted how a 
digital twin of a network of subsea pipelines continuously ana-
lyzes sensor data (pressure, flow, vibration) to detect anomalies 
such as small leaks or fatigue cracks. If a sensor detects a slight 
pressure drop in a pipeline segment, the twin's model might 
localise it to a potential pinhole leak and schedule a targeted ROV 
inspection at that spot rather than a blind full survey. The twin 
can also simulate the pipeline's integrity under different condi -
tions, for example, how it would handle a 50‐year storm or varying 
temperatures to guide preemptive maintenance.
Remote operation is another aspect: many new offshore in-
stallations are designed to be normally unattended facilities 
(NUFs). Their operation is controlled from onshore control cen-
tres, sometimes hundreds of kilometres away. Digital twins serve 
as the virtual interface for operators to manage these remote 
platforms. The twin of a subsea well can show valve states, flow 
rates, and even a 3D visualisation of the equipment on the sea-
floor, allowing engineers to open/close valves or adjust pumps 
remotely with confidence, as if they had an eye on the seabed. This 
mirrors what has been done in mining and space: operate via the 
twin to compensate for not physically being there.
Environmental impact assessment is also aided by digital twins 
underwater. As noted by NSC, a twin can incorporate data on 
water quality, currents, and marine life around a subsea struc -
ture. By simulating potential incidents (such as an oil leak or 
noise from construction) in the twin, companies and regulators 
can predict the impact on the ecosystem and take measures to mitigate harm. This proactive use of twins aligns with sustain -
ability goals (e.g., planning construction timing to avoid marine 
mating seasons by simulating noise propagation in the ocean, 
something a twin can do by coupling acoustic models with real 
hydrophone data) [54–56 ].
3.3|Desert and Remote Land Sites
Deserts such as the Sahara, Australian Outback, or Gobi are 
often chosen for massive projects such as solar farms, mines, or 
pipelines. These areas have extreme heat and dust and are far 
from population centres, making on‐site staffing difficult. In 
such projects, digital twins support primarily remote operations 
and performance optimisation. A solar farm in a desert might 
have a digital twin that collects real‐time data from each panel 
(temperature, output) and from weather stations (sunlight, dust 
levels) to predict maintenance needs such as panel cleaning or 
cooling requirements. With potentially thousands of panels, the 
twin can use AI to identify which sections are underperforming 
due to dust accumulation and then guide autonomous cleaning 
robots or dispatch a small crew directly to those spots [57–59 ].
Mining operations in remote deserts have been early adopters of 
digital twin‐like approaches. Companies in Australia, such as 
Rio Tinto, operate large iron ore mines in the Pilbara desert 
from control centres in Perth (over 1500 km away). They use 
integrated digital models of the mine sites, fed by data from 
autonomous haul trucks, drilling rigs, and railway sensors. 
Essentially, these are digital twins of the mines, complete with 
topography updated by drone surveys and IoT data from ma-
chinery, allowing managers to optimise production and detect 
problems without setting foot in the mine pit. One report 
mentions that top mining firms are ‘pioneering Digital Twin and 
AI strategies to enhance productivity and safety’ in these remote 
mines. The benefits include being able to simulate different 
mine plans (such as digging sequences) in the twin to see which 
yields the best efficiency or to foresee bottlenecks in the system 
(such as a breakdown of a critical truck). Additionally, by 
linking the twin to AI, anomalies such as an overheating truck 
engine can be caught via patterns in the data, and the truck can 
be rerouted to maintenance before it fails in the field. This 
predictive maintenance via twin is directly credited with 
reducing downtime in these remote mines.
A specific example: Rio Tinto's Gudai‐Darri mine (formerly 
Koodaideri) is branded as the company's most technologically 
advanced mine. It uses a ‘digital twin of the mine’ to integrate 
data from all assets and systems, a concept that Rio Tinto ac-
knowledges was inspired by NASA's pioneering use of twins. 
This twin coordinates autonomous drills, trucks, and even a 
robotic train system (AutoHaul). The outcome is that personnel 
can monitor and control the entire mining operation from a 
single ops centre building, with the twin providing situational 
awareness as good as being on‐site.

3.5|Extreme Disaster Response Construction
Another remote scenario is when a disaster (earthquake, hur-
ricane) wipes out infrastructure, and rapid rebuilding is needed 
in a hazardous zone. Teams cannot always be on site (due to 
aftershocks or contamination). Digital twins of the affected area, 
constructed from satellite and drone data, can guide remote‐ 
controlled construction equipment to clear debris or erect 
temporary structures without putting responders in harm's way. 
Although not routine yet, this application has been theorised: 
for example, after a nuclear accident (such as Fukushima), a 
digital twin of the site with radiological maps could be used to 
plan robotic construction of containment walls and remotely 
operate the machinery, minimising human exposure.
Across these examples, the recurring theme is that digital twins 
enable a shift from reactive site‐bound operations to proactive 
remote and data‐driven operations. By having a continuously 
updated virtual replica, organisations can make informed de-
cisions without having eyes physically on the asset 24/7. This is 
transformative for remote construction, where travel to the site 
might be infrequent or dangerous. Furthermore, digital twins 
contribute to sustainability and efficiency in these projects: 
maintenance can be done just in time (reducing unnecessary 
trips or component replacements), operations can be optimised 
for energy use, and the lifespan of assets can be extended by 
carefully monitoring and mitigating deterioration. For instance, 
if a twin shows that reducing a remote diesel generator's load at 
certain times will extend its life by a year, operators can adjust 
accordingly and avoid having to fly in a replacement so soon, 
saving cost and reducing carbon footprint [63–65 ].
It is worth noting that while the technology is promising, imple -
menting digital twins in these environments requires overcoming 
challenges such as sparse connectivity (e.g., Antarctic stations 
have limited bandwidth to send data to their twins in real‐time), 
sensor reliability in harsh conditions and cybersecurity (remote 
control via a twin must be secure against hacking). Nonetheless, 
successful case studies are accumulating, as we will highlight in 
the next section.Beyond the specific contexts of Arctic, subsea, desert, and 
disaster zones, digital twins also offer transformative benefits for 
other remote terrestrial infrastructure systems facing opera -
tional and environmental challenges. For example, digital twins 
are increasingly used in urban critical infrastructure networks, 
such as water distribution systems, power grids, and trans -
portation networks, to improve resilience and continuity under 
climate‐induced risks. Urban Digital Twin (UDT) systems 
integrate real‐time sensor data, predictive analytics, and simu -
lation models to support decision‐making under uncertainty, 
optimise resource allocation, and enhance service delivery 
across extended networks vulnerable to floods, heat waves, or 
supply disruptions [66]. Studies show that digital twins can 
significantly improve resilience and sustainability in critical 
infrastructure such as utility networks by enabling real‐time 
anomaly detection and scenario planning, which reduces 
downtime and service interruptions [36]. Moreover, infrastruc -
ture digital twins in smart cities are being used to forecast social 
and environmental stresses (e.g., anticipating load peaks or 
infrastructure failures), demonstrating that remote terrestrial 
use cases are not limited to isolated sites but extend to net-
worked urban‐regional systems that are complex, geographi -
cally dispersed, and subject to systemic risks [67].
4|Global Case Studies
To illustrate how digital twin technology is being applied in 
both space and remote terrestrial construction, we present a 
range of case studies from around the world involving govern -
ment agencies, space organisations, and industry. These exam -
ples demonstrate the real‐world impact and lessons learnt from 
deploying digital twins in extreme environment projects:
4.1|NASA—Artemis Programme and Lunar 
Infrastructure
NASA has integrated digital twin concepts throughout the 
Artemis lunar exploration programme. Case in point: Artemis 
Lunar Base Planning, NASA's analogue studies for a lunar base 
(such as NASA Langley's habitat testbeds) rely on digital twins 
to iterate designs. They created high‐fidelity digital models of 
habitats and rovers, incorporating lunar environmental data, to 
decide optimal layouts for Artemis Base Camp. Additionally, 
NASA's Lunar VIPER rover (set to prospect lunar ice) has a full 
digital twin for mission simulation; engineers at Ames Research 
Center use it to simulate driving routes and drilling operations 
in lunar soil before the rover goes to the Moon, reducing risk. 
Strategically, NASA sees digital twins as crucial for sustaining 
long‐term lunar operations where Earth contact is limited. 
Policy‐wise, NASA's leadership has been evangelising digital 
twins; for example, in 2021, NASA released a comprehensive 
plan (NASA Technical Memo 20220018538) outlining how 
digital twins, combined with AI and IoT, will enable ‘faster, 
more agile processes’ and safer missions [68].
An illustrative deep‐dive into NASA's use of digital twins is 
found in its critical infrastructure and aerospace operations. In 
the context of space missions and critical infrastructure, digital

4.2|European Space Agency (ESA)—Analogue 
Testing and ‘Digital Twin Earth’
ESA's contributions span both space construction and Earth 
system twins. On the space side, ESA has run analogue field 
trials (e.g., Pangaea and Luna simulations) where astronauts 
and robots perform mock lunar construction tasks; these trials 
use digital twins of equipment and sites to evaluate operations. 
On Earth, ESA is spearheading the ‘Digital Twin Earth’ project 
under the EU's Destination Earth initiative. Although not about 
construction, Digital Twin Earth is a platform simulating 
Earth's climate and natural systems at high fidelity to inform 
climate resilience strategies, a concept directly relevant to 
designing resilient infrastructure. Insights from Digital Twin 
Earth (e.g., predicting sea‐level rise or extreme weather pat-
terns) feed into how we should build future coastal or Arctic 
infrastructure. This demonstrates cross‐pollination: space 
agencies are applying their digital twin expertise gained from 
spacecraft to broader planetary management and infrastructure 
policy, effectively treating Earth itself as a ‘remote environment’ 
that needs a twin for better stewardship [69].
4.3|Japan (JAXA) and Komatsu—Autonomous 
Lunar Construction Project
As mentioned earlier, Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, 
Transport, and Tourism (MLIT) initiated a multi‐company 
project for Autonomous Construction in Space (the ‘Stardust 
Programme’). Komatsu, a leading construction machinery 
manufacturer, was selected for its proposal to use digital twins 
on the Moon. In practice, Komatsu is developing a suite of 
construction equipment (bulldozers, excavators) that can oper -
ate on the lunar surface. The digital twin component involves 
creating a virtual lunar worksite with realistic physics. Komatsu 
has been testing controlling earth‐moving robots in a simulated 
lunar terrain (including latency to mimic the Moon‐Earth delay) 
via their digital twins, and initial results showed operators could 
grade soil remotely with only virtual feedback. This case study is 
significant because it is a public‐private partnership: JAXA (Japan's space agency) provides lunar data and requirements, 
MLIT provides funding and integration to national space strat -
egy, and Komatsu provides technology and machinery know‐ 
how. It shows how policy and industry can align; MLIT sees 
value not just for space but also in how the tech can rebound to 
benefit remote construction in Japan (e.g., operating construc -
tion machines in disaster zones or hazardous areas via twins). 
This programme thus has dual‐use: advancing lunar base ca-
pabilities and modernising domestic construction automation 
[42, 43].
4.4|SpaceX—Starship Development and Mars 
Mission Simulation
SpaceX, a private company, has aggressively used digital simu -
lations which effectively function as digital twins. The Starship 
vehicle (intended for Moon and Mars missions) is so large and 
novel that SpaceX cannot test everything physically. Instead, per 
reports, SpaceX created a digital twin of Starship to simulate its 
entire flight envelope and even a hypothetical Mars mission. A 
2024 scientific paper noted that SpaceX's team runs simulations 
‘based on publicly released data’ to validate Starship's design for 
a human Mars mission, including interplanetary trajectory and 
entry, descent, and landing sequences. In one instance, SpaceX's 
twin revealed structural issues in ground tests, prompting 
design tweaks before the next prototype was built, saving time 
and money (this is partially evidenced by the relatively quick 
iterations of Starship prototypes). Another facet: SpaceX's 
launch facilities at Boca Chica, Texas, are heavily instrumented 
and modelled; after the first Starship orbital launch attempt 
caused pad damage, SpaceX used a digital twin of the pad and 
rocket plume to redesign a new water‐cooled flame diverter for 
the launch mount. By simulating the next launch with this twin, 
they improved the pad's resilience. SpaceX's case underlines the 
private sector's willingness to rely on digital twins for rapid 
innovation. It also hints at how a Mars base by SpaceX might be 
managed. Elon Musk has mentioned having ‘ops centres on 
Earth controlling things on Mars’, which implies extensive 
digital twin infrastructure to supervise those distant opera -
tions [70].
4.5|ispace and Other NewSpace Companies
ispace, a Japanese startup, attempted a lunar landing in 2023 
(Mission 1). Although not much is publicly disclosed about their 
use of digital twins, it is known that they ran extensive simu -
lations of the lander's descent and had a mission control that 
visualised the lander's state in real‐time. We can infer that a 
digital twin of the lander (with its dynamics) was used to 
practice the landing sequence and adjust parameters. Although 
the lander ultimately crashed, the company stated they would 
analyse the data via their simulation models to pinpoint what 
went wrong, essentially using the twin for post‐incident forensic 
analysis, similar to how Apollo 13's twin was used. This pattern 
is common: when new space companies (such as ispace, 
Astrobotic, etc.) operate at lower budgets, digital twins offer a 
cost‐effective means to test and troubleshoot without needing 
multiple expensive physical prototypes [71].

The UK has been a thought leader in applying digital twins for 
national infrastructure. Through the Centre for Digital Built 
Britain (CDBB) and initiatives such as the Gemini Principles,

Although this is more policy and strategy than a single project, it 
has led to several pilot case studies: one involves a digital twin of

twin of an offshore wind farm to maximise energy output and 
schedule maintenance during favourable weather windows. For 
remote construction relevance, the UK's effort demonstrates 
how multiple twins can link. For example, a twin of an energy 
grid can connect with a twin of a remote wind farm and a twin 
of weather systems to form a holistic view. Policy‐wise, the UK's 
approach addresses data sharing and interoperability, which is 
crucial if, say, a space habitat twin should interface with Earth‐

with infrastructure twins to plan protective construction [72].

development of Urban Digital Twins (UDTs) for resilient utility 
systems. Ref. [66] shows that UDTs integrate real‐time sensor 
feeds, advanced simulations, and predictive analytics to support 
decision‐making across water, energy, and transportation net-
works under climate stress. For example, digital twins of urban 
water distribution networks facilitate instant monitoring of hy-
draulic behaviour and enable intelligent response strategies to 
pipe failures or demand surges before service disruption occurs, 
enhancing reliability in the face of extreme events. Similarly, 
digital twin frameworks for smart grids allow utilities to simulate 
fault propagation and optimise load balancing under variable 
environmental conditions, which is critical for networks covering 
remote and high‐latitude regions [67]. These detailed cases 
demonstrate that digital twin implementations can transition 
from asset‐centric systems to network‐wide resilience platforms, 
offering continuous adaptive capacity for distributed critical 
infrastructure essential to communities under stress.
4.7|Canada—Mining and Northern 
Infrastructure
Canadian companies and researchers have deployed digital 
twins in mining (similar to Australia) and are exploring twins 
for northern roads. Case: Diavik Diamond Mine (Northwest 
Territories): This Arctic mine uses a digital twin to integrate 
real‐time data from its open‐pit and underground operations 
with its ore processing plant. The twin helps coordinate 
equipment and predict maintenance, which is vital as the mine 
is accessible by an ice road only part of the year. By knowing 
exactly which truck or drill needs servicing and when, Diavik 
can ensure parts are brought in on the annual ice road or flights, 
avoiding emergency fixes in winter. Another Canadian project is 
the SMARTICE programme, which uses a digital twin of sea‐ice 
conditions along Arctic community ice roads (these are seasonal 
roads over frozen sea used to truck supplies). The twin assimi -
lates satellite imagery and sensor measurements to map ice thickness and strength, predicting when and where the ice road 
will become unsafe. This directly informs the construction of 
alternate land routes or bridges to maintain supply lines as 
climate warms, showcasing a fusion of indigenous knowledge, 
IoT, and twin technology for resilience [73].
4.8|Industry—Shell's Digital Twin for Offshore 
Platforms
Royal Dutch Shell developed a comprehensive digital twin of its 
massive Prelude floating LNG platform (offshore Australia), the 
largest floating structure ever built. This twin includes a 3D 
model of the entire facility and real‐time data from tens of 
thousands of sensors. During operations, Shell's engineers 
(often onshore) navigate the twin in VR to inspect equipment 
‘virtually’ instead of doing risky on‐platform inspections. In one 
instance, when a compressor fault occurred, engineers used the 
twin's historical data and simulation capability to identify the 
cause and fix it with minimal downtime, saving millions in lost 
production. Prelude's twin is an example of scale: it is similar to 
a mini‐city twin on the ocean. Shell has reported that lessons 
from Prelude's twin allow them to design future platforms with 
better placement of sensors and design for maintainability [74].
These case studies collectively underline several points. First, 
digital twin adoption is inherently interdisciplinary and often 
requires collaborations (space agencies with manufacturers, 
governments with tech firms). Second, the value of digital twins 
has been proven in both prevention and response, preventing 
failures (JWST, pipelines, wind farms) and responding to anom -
alies (Apollo 13, ispace crash analysis, oil rig faults) more effec -
tively. Third, policy frameworks such as the UK's or Japan's 
indicate that governments are recognising digital twins as stra-
tegic infrastructure in their own right, deserving investment akin 
to physical infrastructure because of the insights they provide.
For policymakers, these examples provide evidence that sup-
porting digital twin development (through funding, open data 
policies, standard‐setting) can yield safer, more resilient, and 
efficient outcomes in construction projects. For industry leaders, 
the cases demonstrate competitive advantages, companies that 
leverage twins (SpaceX, Rio Tinto, Shell) can move faster, safer, 
and often cheaper by anticipating issues and optimising 
operations.
In extreme environment construction, where uncertainty is 
high, these advantages become even more critical. A lunar base 
or a polar railway cannot afford trial‐and‐error to the same 
degree as a temperate project; having a digital twin acts as a 
high‐tech insurance policy and guide. The next section will 
build on this by discussing strategic and policy implications in a 
broader sense, considering how to maximise the benefits 
observed in these cases (Figure 5).
5|Strategic and Policy Implications for Future 
Infrastructure Resilience and Sustainability

5.2|Sustainability and Efficient Resource 
Utilisation
By optimising operations and maintenance, digital twins 
contribute to sustainability goals. They help extend the lifespan of 
assets (through timely maintenance and avoiding catastrophic 
failures that lead to rebuilds) and reduce resource waste. For 
instance, twins can optimise energy usage in remote facilities 
(ensuring generators run at optimal load and coordinating 
renewable energy sources with demand). They can also minimise 
unnecessary travel, if you can inspect virtually, you avoid flying a 
helicopter to an oil rig or driving to a pipeline pump station, 
cutting carbon emissions. At scale, this means a smaller envi -
ronmental footprint for maintaining infrastructure. Policymakers 
focused on decarbonisation can leverage this: they could provide 
incentives (such as tax breaks or innovation grants) to infra -
structure operators who implement digital twins proven to save 
energy or reduce emissions. Additionally, digital twins can help 
with circular economy principles. By tracking the condition of 
materials and components, they facilitate refurbishing or recy -
cling infrastructure parts at end‐of‐life rather than scrapping 
them blindly. For example, a twin might reveal that certain 
modules of a Mars habitat can be safely reused for another 
mission, informing sustainable mission design [77, 78].
5.3|Data Governance, Standards, and 
Interoperability
Effective digital twin ecosystems require robust data gover -
nance. Twins integrate data across sectors. Consider a remote 
mining twin that might benefit from satellite weather data or a 
space habitat twin that might need inputs from an Earth‐based 
medical database for astronaut health. Sharing such data raises 
issues of privacy, security, and interoperability. Strategically, 
there is a need for standards to allow different digital twins 
(maybe owned by various entities) to connect. The UK's Infor -
mation Management Framework (IMF) for connected digital 
twins is a leading example, setting out semantic standards and 
protocols for data exchange. Internationally, organisations such 
as ISO and IEEE are working on digital twin standards (e.g., ISO 
23247 [79] for digital twin in manufacturing). Governments can 
accelerate this by adopting open standards in their projects and 
requiring vendors to comply, thus avoiding vendor lock‐in and 
siloed systems. Interoperability is especially crucial in remote 
scenarios involving multiple stakeholders: for instance, a polar 
observatory's twin might need to interface with a national 
climate twin, or a lunar base twin might integrate data from 
modules built by different countries. A common framework 
(akin to the internet protocols for data) ensures all these twins 
can ‘talk’ to each other in a secure, meaningful way. Trust 
frameworks (such as the Gemini Principles emphasising trust, 
security, and public benefit) are also needed so that organisa -
tions are willing to share data from their twins when it serves a 
greater resilience goal [80, 81].5.4|Investment in Digital Infrastructure
Viewing digital twins as part of infrastructure implies that na-
tions should invest in them similarly to physical infrastructure. 
This includes funding for high‐performance computing, 
communication networks, and sensor deployments that make 
twins possible. For example, to have a digital twin of the entire 
national grid, a country may need to install thousands of extra 
sensors and a robust fibre‐optic network to carry the data. These 
have costs that can be justified by the benefits of preventing 
blackouts or enabling more renewable integration via the twin's 
insights. In remote construction, an investment might be 
needed for satellite or 5G communications to ensure the twin of, 
say, a remote dam can update in real‐time despite its location. 
There is also a human capital investment: training engineers 
and operators to work with digital twins (data analytics skills, 
familiarity with simulation tools). Policymakers could 
encourage curricula that blend civil engineering with data sci-
ence and perhaps create exchange programs between aerospace 
experts and civil infrastructure experts to cross‐pollinate 
knowledge on twins [75, 82].
5.5|Public‐Private Collaboration and Knowledge 
Sharing
Many digital twin successes have come from one‐off projects 
(e.g., JWST, Rio Tinto's mine), and often that knowledge is not 
widely disseminated. To avoid reinventing the wheel, there is a 
need for better knowledge sharing. Governments and industry 
bodies can organise forums or working groups specifically on 
digital twins for extreme environments, where lessons learnt 
can be discussed. NASA, for instance, might share methodolo -
gies with mining companies on how they validate twin accu -
racy, or vice versa (mining might share how they manage twin 
operations with intermittent comms, which could help space 
missions). Public‐private partnerships can also de‐risk the initial 
development of digital twins for new domains, as Japan did with 
Komatsu, and as the EU does via Horizon Europe funding for 
digital twin pilots. By co‐funding these initiatives, the public 
sector ensures the results are openly available to benefit all 
(e.g., requiring open results or software tools from the projects). 
One strategic recommendation is establishing a ‘Digital Twin 
Extreme Environments Consortium’ globally, involving space 
agencies, military engineers (who build in extreme places such 
as Afghanistan mountains or ocean floors), civil engineers, and 
tech companies, to develop best practices and perhaps open‐ 
source toolkits for twins [83, 84].

physical systems, questions arise about liability and regulation.

twin shuts down a pipeline to prevent damage) and that action 
causes financial loss or fails to prevent an accident, who is 
responsible, the operator or the software developer? Regulatory 
frameworks will need updating to account for AI‐driven and 
digital twin‐driven operations. Verification and validation of

5.7|Ethical and Social Implications
With increased monitoring (such as sensors everywhere feeding 
twins), there could be privacy issues, especially if the infra -
structure involves communities (smart city twins, etc.). Even in 
remote construction, consider that a twin might monitor 
workers via wearables for safety (common in modern con-
struction). Policies must ensure that the data collected is used 
ethically and with consent. Additionally, workforce implica -
tions: some fear that heavy automation via digital twins could 
reduce certain jobs (such as on‐site inspectors or equipment 
operators). Policymakers should accompany twin rollouts with 
retraining programs so that those workers can transition to new 
roles such as twin supervisors, analysts, or robot operators. 
These roles are often safer and higher‐skilled. The narrative 
should be straightforward that digital twins augment human 
decision‐making rather than replace it; this framing can help 
with public acceptance, particularly in government projects 
[87, 88].
In conclusion, on policy, embracing digital twin technology for 
space and remote construction aligns with broader objectives of 
building resilient, sustainable, and smart infrastructure. Countries that move early to integrate twins into their infra -
structure strategy may gain a significant advantage in managing 
the impacts of climate change and in carrying out ambitious 
projects (such as space exploration or Arctic development) 
safely. The transformative potential of twins justifies treating 
data and simulations as critical infrastructure themselves. As 
one report succinctly put it, connected digital twins are tools to 
understand the complexities of interconnected systems and pro-
vide better insights for decisions . In our context, those inter -
connected systems span Earth and space. By making digital 
twins a strategic priority, policymakers ensure that as we push 
the frontiers, building cities on the Moon or wind farms at the 
poles, we do so with eyes wide open, informed by the best vir-
tual foresight technology can offer.
6|Future Trends and Emerging Technologies
Looking ahead, the landscape of digital twins for construction in 
extreme environments is set to evolve rapidly, propelled by 
advances in related technologies. Several emerging trends are 
likely to shape the next 5–10 years, making digital twins even 
more powerful and indispensable (Figure 7):
6.1|Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine 
Learning Integration
AI is poised to become the ‘brain’ of digital twin systems, 
handling complex predictions and autonomous decision‐ 
making. Currently, many twins use relatively deterministic 
physics models and rule‐based thresholds for alerts. In the 
future, machine learning algorithms (trained on the vast data 
that twins accumulate) will identify patterns and optimise op-
erations in ways humans might not notice. For example, an AI

also run countless simulation scenarios (Monte Carlo simula -
tions) automatically and use reinforcement learning to improve 
control strategies for robots or processes. NASA indicates that as 
AI/ML mature and integrate, ‘the digital twin will play a more 
central role in space missions, especially in remote operations and 
complex system management’, essentially because AI can help 
handle non‐deterministic, unexpected situations autonomously. 
We can envision a Mars base twin that has an AI assistant 
continuously optimising life support settings for efficiency and 
longevity, or a pipeline twin that uses ML to accurately predict 
corrosion growth under varying conditions far better than 
manual models. However, with AI comes the need for trans -
parency. An ongoing area of research will be explainable AI for 
digital twins, ensuring that when an AI in the loop suggests a 
critical action (such as ‘evacuate habitat Section 3’), it can 
explain which sensor readings or model inferences led to that 
suggestion [3, 89].
6.2|Advanced Robotics and Swarm Automation
The future of construction in extreme environments will likely 
involve robotic teams rather than single machines. Swarm ro-
botics, large numbers of smaller robots working collaboratively, 
draw inspiration from social insects and promise redundancy 
and efficiency. Digital twins will be crucial for coordinating and 
monitoring swarms. Researchers are already exploring how 
digital twin platforms can handle communication and task 
allocation among robot swarms. A digital twin can act as the 
central hive mind, aggregating data from all robots and then 
sending back optimised instructions to each. For instance, in 
building a lunar structure, a swarm of 50 small rovers could 
continuously feed their position and status into the twin; the 
twin runs an algorithm to decide which rover should dig where 
or carry which block, and updates the plan in real‐time as 
conditions change (such as one rover goes offline). This dy-
namic reconfiguration through the twin increases resilience, 
and even if several robots fail, the twin adapts the swarm's tasks 
so that construction continues. We already see early imple -
mentations: NASA has concepts for swarms assembling struc -
tures in orbit (e.g., swarms of free‐flying bots building a large 
antenna), and Europe has done lab tests with micro‐robots 
assembling blocks, guided by a central computer vision system 
(a form of twin). As swarm tech grows, standards might emerge 
for ‘swarm‐twin interfaces’. Additionally, robotics advance -
ments such as soft robots or shape‐shifting robots will provide 
new data to twins (imagine a flexible robot whose shape can 
change; its twin must incorporate a physics model for that 
flexibility). The twin will thus evolve to handle more complex 
robot kinematics and collaborative behaviours [47, 90].
6.3|Extended Reality (XR) and Human‐Twin 
Interaction
The way humans interact with digital twins will become more 
immersive and intuitive. We expect much greater use of Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Mixed Reality to 
visualise and control twins. In a VR environment, an engineer 
could virtually ‘walk through’ a lunar habitat's twin, inspecting 
systems as if present on the Moon. Already, companies such as 
Unity and NVIDIA's Omniverse are developing 3D platforms for 
digital twins that support VR viewing of factory twins or city 
twins. In construction, this means project stakeholders (even 
policymakers) could put on VR goggles and experience a remote 
project's status via its twin, improving communication and 
understanding. AR, on the other hand, will assist on‐site crews. 
For example, a maintenance technician at a remote site could 
wear AR glasses that overlay the digital twin's info onto what 
they see, showing which buried cable segment exactly needs 
repair or highlighting an anomaly in a structure that is invisible 
to the naked eye but detected by the twin. In space, astronauts 
might rely on AR fed by the twin to perform repairs (there is 
talk of using Microsoft's HoloLens on the ISS for this type of 
guidance). XR also opens up multi‐user collaboration in the 
twin—teams across the globe can meet ‘inside’ the digital twin 
model to discuss changes or issues, almost similar to a virtual 
conference at the construction site. This will be incredibly 
useful for international projects (e.g., a multinational Moon base 
programme) to make collective decisions efficiently [92, 93].
6.4|Smart Materials and IoT Sensor Proliferation
The fidelity and utility of digital twins depend on data, which 
relies on sensors. We anticipate both an increase in the quantity 
of sensors (cheaper, smaller IoT devices scattered through 
structures) and the quality of sensors (new types that can 
measure things we could not before). Smart materials are ma-
terials that have built‐in sensing or actuation. For example, 
concrete can sense its own strain or ‘self‐heal’ cracks with 
embedded microcapsules. When such materials are used, their 
behaviour can be directly integrated into the twin. If a habitat is 
built from smart composite panels that change colour under 
stress (as a crude sensor), cameras can feed that info to the 
twin's structural model to indicate stress distribution. More 
advanced: self‐healing materials might tell the twin how much 
of their healing capacity is left, so the twin knows when the 
material might need external intervention. By incorporating 
these, the twin becomes more accurate and can even simulate 
the active response of materials. A digital twin with smart ma-
terial feedback could potentially initiate repairs, for example, 
send a current to a smart rebar grid to heat and heal cracks as 
soon as the twin detects micro‐cracking. Thus, the line between 
the twin and the physical asset blurs as materials become part of 
the cyber‐physical loop.
Furthermore, tiny energy‐harvesting sensors could be sprinkled 
in places previously unmonitored (such as deep in a glacier 
supporting a structure or throughout a spacecraft's skin). This 
proliferation of IoT will feed the twin big data, which loops back 
to the need for AI to make sense of it. An exciting development 
is satellite IoT constellations, low earth orbit satellites that 
collect data from remote IoT sensors anywhere on Earth. This 
means even the most isolated construction site can pipe data to 
its twin in the cloud in near real‐time without having to build 
local comms infrastructure. It is a synergy of space tech aiding 
terrestrial twins [65, 94].

Ecosystems
We touched on connected twins, and the future likely holds not 
just individual twins, but large ecosystems or federations of 
twins. For example, a ‘Mars colony twin’ might consist of many 
sub‐twins (life support, habitat structures, power grid, rover 
fleet) that all interface. Managing such complexity will require 
hierarchical or distributed twin architectures. Techniques such 
as agent‐based modelling could allow each sub‐twin to be an 
agent that communicates with others. The Gemini Principles in 
the UK foresee an ‘ecosystem of connected digital twins’ where 
data flows securely between domains. Imagine a scenario in 
2040: multiple lunar bases by different countries exist; each has 
its digital twin. For global safety, these twins might share certain 
data (such as the location of all active rovers to avoid interfer -
ence or environmental readings). Thus, an Internet of Digital 
Twins might emerge, analogous to today's Internet of com -
puters. Policies and technology will need to allow selective, on‐ 
demand connectivity. For example, a twin can request info from 
another twin about a shared resource (such as water usage in a 
shared crater). Cloud platforms are already gearing up for this 
kind of thing, for example, Microsoft Azure and AWS have 
‘digital twin’ services aiming to host multiple twin instances 
that can connect via APIs. On Earth, smart cities might link 
their twins for mutual benefit (a twin of a power plant linking 
with the city twin to manage distribution, etc.). All of this points 
to scale: from single assets to system‐of‐systems twins, covering 
entire operations or even regions [72, 96].
6.6|Increased Autonomy and Closed‐Loop 
Construction
Ultimately, the goal in very remote or dangerous environments is 
fully autonomous construction, machines building with minimal 
human intervention. Digital twins will serve as the central control 
system for such autonomy. A futuristic example: a crewless 
mission lands on Mars with robotic builders and supplies. A 
digital twin of the entire mission (hosted maybe on an orbiting 
satellite for computational power) orchestrates the construction 
of a habitat before humans arrive. It processes data, commands 
robots, and deals with surprises (similar to a dust storm) by re‐ 
planning, essentially acting as an AI site foreman. Although 
this level of autonomy might be a couple of decades out, the 
components are being put in place: AI planning, robust hardware, 
and high‐fidelity simulations to validate actions. The twins would 
constantly simulate and verify the robots' work as they pro-
gressed, making corrections on the fly. Success in such an 
endeavour would rewrite construction manuals and would likely 
trickle back to Earth for automated construction in hazardous 
sites (imagine the cleanup of a radioactive site entirely by 
autonomous robots guided by a twin) [15, 97].
6.7|Quantum Computing and High‐Fidelity 
Physics
Looking even further, as quantum computing matures, it could 
vastly improve the simulation aspect of digital twins. Complex physics (fluid dynamics, material quantum effects, etc.) that are 
too slow to simulate in real‐time today might become tractable. 
This means twins could incorporate much more detailed phys -
ics, for example, precisely simulating how lunar dust will levi-
tate and coat surfaces during rocket landings (a very complex 
plasma physics problem) to design better dust mitigation mea -
sures for habitats. High‐fidelity twins may also allow real‐time 
optimisation (such as shape changes to structures in response 
to loads). If smart materials allow structures to adapt, a 
quantum‐enhanced twin could compute the optimal structural 
reconfiguration in real‐time during, say, an earthquake, and 
instruct the structure to adjust accordingly. Although specula -
tive, this hints at a future where the line between simulation 
and reality is extremely thin. The twin is so accurate and so 
tightly coupled that it essentially becomes the control system for 
the structure continuously [98, 99].
In summary, the future of digital twins in space and remote 
construction is deeply intertwined with other cutting‐edge tech. 
AI will make twins smarter; robotics will make them more ac-
tion‐oriented; XR will make them more user‐friendly; smart 
materials and IoT will make them richer in data; and connec -
tivity will make them more collaborative. All these trends 
reinforce each other. Importantly, this future is not just about 
technology for its own sake. It serves a vision of creating 
infrastructure that is self‐aware, self‐optimising, and self‐ 
healing to the greatest extent possible. That vision is particu -
larly pertinent in environments where human intervention is 
costly or impossible.
7|Conclusion
Digital twin technology is transitioning from a novel concept to 
a practical necessity for tackling some of the most challenging 
construction and infrastructure projects faced by humanity. In 
extreme environments, whether the airless plains of the Moon, 
the dusty Martian horizon, the crushing depths of the ocean, or 
the frigid expanse of polar tundra, digital twins provide a critical 
bridge between the physical and the digital, between presence 
and remoteness, between the known and the unknown. This 
article has examined how digital twins function as high‐fidelity 
virtual counterparts to physical systems, and how they are being 
applied to re‐imagine construction and maintenance in envi -
ronments where traditional approaches fall short. By enabling 
continuous monitoring, simulation‐driven planning, and even 
remote control of assets, digital twins amplify our ability to 
build and sustain resilient infrastructure in places we cannot 
always be.
For multidisciplinary engineers and policymakers alike, the 
message is clear: embracing digital twins is a strategic move 
towards smarter, safer, and more sustainable infrastructure. The 
case studies from NASA, ESA, JAXA, SpaceX, and global in-
dustry leaders demonstrate that digital twins are already pre-
venting failures, improving efficiencies, and saving costs in pilot 
projects around the world. The next step is scaling these suc-
cesses through supportive policies, standardisation of best 
practices, and continued research and development. Policy -
makers have a role in incentivising the integration of digital 
twins into critical infrastructure and ensuring the frameworks

When planning a lunar base or a new Arctic highway today,

Looking forward, as technology continues to advance, the syn-
ergy of digital twins with AI, robotics, XR, and novel materials 
will unlock capabilities that today might seem similar to science 
fiction, fully autonomous construction sites, ecosystems of 
interconnected twins optimising entire cities or planets, and 
perhaps even digital twins of humans (astronaut avatars) 
working side by side with physical robots. Each of these de-
velopments will further reduce the gap between what is imag -

construction. The transformative potential is immense: we will 
be able to ‘live’ in these distant or inhospitable environments 
virtually, solving problems and refining designs in real‐time, 
and then confidently execute those solutions in the physical 
world.
In conclusion, digital twins are not just a tool but a paradigm 
shift in engineering and infrastructure management. They 
embody the convergence of the digital revolution with the age‐ 
old practice of building in the physical world. For extreme 
environment construction, this convergence offers a lifeline, a 
way to proceed with ambition but also with caution, informed 
by data and foresight. As we embark on building out the final 
frontier of space and securing the most remote corners of Earth, 
digital twins will be our reliable partners, illuminating the path, 
predicting the pitfalls, and helping us create structures that 
stand the test of time and nature. The journey is just beginning, 
but the trajectory is set: the future of construction, especially 
where it is hardest, will be co‐created by engineers and their 
digital twins. By harnessing this technology wisely, we can turn 
bold visions, lunar habitats, underwater colonies, and resilient 
polar networks into reality, sustainably, and resiliently, for the 
benefit of all.
