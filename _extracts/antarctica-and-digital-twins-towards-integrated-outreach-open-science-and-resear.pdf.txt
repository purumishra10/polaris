Perspective
Antarctica and digital twins: towards integrated outreach, open
science and research station management
Javier Menéndez-Blázquez
ICBiBE - Cavanilles Institute of Biodiversity and Evolutionary Biology, University of Valencia, Spain
Abstract
Antarctica constitutes a natural laboratory dedicated exclusively to peaceful and scientific purposes. In this context, Antarctic research
stations represent the operational hubs for scientific activity and the primary sites of human presence on the continent. However, theirremoteness, logistical complexity and strong seasonality constrain public visibility, accessibility and the monitoring of scientific operations .
This perspective paper explores the potential of spatial digital twins (SDTs) as integrative tools to address these limitations and proposing a
conceptual framework for their application in Antarctic research stations. This framework leverages virtual platforms for the operational and
scientific monitoring of infrastructures, as well as for open data dissemination, decision support and improved accessibility, in line with theprinciples of the Antarctic Treaty. By synthesizing spatial data, sensor networks and real-time streams, SDTs enable the continuous monitoringof complex socio-technical systems. Drawing on their transformative role in fields such as urban planning and industrial management,
SDTs can enhance operational management, scientific tracking and open data dissemination. Finally, the discussion addresses the potential
technical, logistical and institutional challenges associated with their implementation into Antarctic research stations, such as data availabi lity,
cost-benefit balancing, standardization, scalability for large-scale stations and long-term maintenance. Despite these hurdles, SDTs have thepotential to become a valuable and scalable standard for monitoring, managing and communicating scientific activity in one of the mostremote and scientifically significant regions on the planet.
Keywords: Antarctic research; Geographic Information Systems (GIS); open research; polar regions; science communication; spatial data

Spatial digital twins
Digital twins are dynamic computational models that replicate
real-world systems, physical environments and their interactionswith other elements, continuously integrating observational datato reflect their state and evolution in near-real time (Crespi et al.
2023). When these models are built upon a Geographic Informa-
tion System (GIS) framework, they are referred to as ‘GIS-based’ ,‘geospatial’ or ‘spatial digital twins’ (hereinafter SDTs; Lehner &Dorffner 2020), enabling the visualization and analysis of spatial
information across multiple dimensions (2D/3D). SDTs allow the

Digital twins in Antarctic research stations
Translating this technology to Antarctic research stations requires
a tailored architecture that addresses their structural and opera-tional diversity, as well as the connectivity provided by research
and logistical vessels, which act as vital mobile nodes within the
broader Antarctic network ( Fig. 1 ). In practice, this system is
envisioned as a web-based geospatial platform that serves as theintegrative hub for the entire digital ecosystem (e.g. ArcGIS Enter-prise, TerriaJS, etc.). Far from being a single piece of software, the
SDT architecture is composed of different modular components:
1) a data-integration and storage layer for harvesting real-timesensor feeds and heterogeneous d atasets (e.g. using FIWARE or
Apache Kafka platforms for ingestion and PostgreSQL/PostGIS
for data management), 2) a GIS-based interface for spatial data
management (as mentioned previously), 3) modular dashboardsfor real-time monitoring and data visualization and 4) interactive3D and Building Information Modelling (BIM) modules forinfrastructure representation (e.g. ESRI ecosystem, CesiumJS,
Autodesk Platform Services, Geolibre.app, etc.; Figs 2 &3). Fur-
thermore, this web-based environment functions as a centralizedknowledge repository, enabling direct integration of externalproject links, real-time research documentation and multimediavisualization tools, among other benefits. This modular framework
addresses the aims of the SDTs, serving as a dynamic interface
for the seamless integration of energy systems, logistical flowsand scientific datasets, providing a unified environment for bothinternal station management and public outreach.
Science outreach and dissemination
The implementation of SDTs in an Antarctic research station
should be rooted in the fundamental pillar of scientific outreach,enabling both the general public and the scientific community toaccess real-time information regarding ongoing activities. A SDTcan serve as a valuable outreach hub by integrating the basic geo-
graphical context with detailed data from diverse sources ( Fig. 2),
effectively bridging the gap between site location, ongoing researchprojects and related scientific findings for a broader audience.For example, the yearly Spanish Army penguin sponsor campaign

Challenges and limitations
Despite their potential and multiple applications, the implemen-
tation of SDTs in Antarctic environments faces several challenges,many of which are inherent to their remoteness and directly affect
real-time monitoring applications. Limited connectivity, high
energy demands, physical maintenance constraints and the riskof sensor disconnection during the winter, when many bases andmuch infrastructure remain closed, significantly impact systemreliability. Furthermore, the diversity of the polar programmes
involved, the research stations and the technological systems and
architectures could complicate the establishment of a single andstandardized SDT model. The Spanish Juan Carlos I Antarcticresearch station has been used here as an illustrative example
of the broader applicability of this approach due to its historical
relevance, operational capacity and recent modernization, amongother characteristics. The information presented is intentionallygeneric and does not aim to accurately represent the current stateof the station but rather to highlight the broader applicability of the
proposed SDT approach to other Antarctic research stations. Thus,
standardization of data formats, exchange protocols and monitor-ing methodologies is crucial to ensuring interoperability amongdifferent actors. In this context, the data hosted within a SDT
could involve the management of sensitive information, requiring
robust protocols against security vulnerabilities (Evangeline 2025),
thereby guaranteeing transparency without compromising theoutreach aims and operational security.
In addition, a primary barrier to SDT implementation is
the acquisition bottleneck, particularly the reliance on manual
inputs for operational workflows. Although automated sensornetworks effectively capture environmental data, the human-in-the-loop requirement for logistical and activity-based records
remains a significant hurdle. While the proposed framework is
well-suited for small- to medium-sized research stations withdefined operational scopes, scaling this system to larger, morecomplex infrastructure presents substantial challenges. To remainviable, SDT development must prioritize high-level automation
by leveraging existing digital logs and interoperable software to
minimize the administrative burden on station staff. In small-to medium-sized research stations, every staff member is acritical resource; therefore, relying on manual data entry not
only is inefficient but also creates a direct trade-off with scientific
personnel capacity. To bridge this gap, the system should pivotfrom manual human-input workflows towards autonomous dataacquisition. A compelling example of this shift is the automationof human occupancy modelling highlighted earlier - a novel
application proposed for SDTs in terms of both public outreach and
the management of scientific activities (see Fig. 2). By integrating
diverse movement tracking technologies, such as indoor LiDAR-based positioning (Karki et al.2024), localized proximity sensors
(e.g. radio frequency identification; RFID) for internal zones
and satellite-based geolocation for field operations, the system
can autonomously monitor human occupancy. This approachcontinuously tracks personnel across both interior facilities andremote field sites, replacing manual reporting with real-time data.
This significantly improves the spatial mapping of human activity,
which is essential for ensuring the protection of critical areas andthe biodiversity surrounding research stations (Salinas etal.2024).
Nonetheless, a specific cost-benefit analysis on the research
station scale is essential, weighing initial outlays against long-term
gains in safety, logistical optimization and scientific transference.
From an institutional perspective, this investment should not beviewed as an isolated expenditure but as a strategy to reduceadministrative overheads and enhance operational resilience.
By prioritizing a modular architecture, national polar programmes
can ensure incremental value gains at every stage, effectivelymitigating the risk of prohibitive upfront costs. Crucially, thisfinancial commitment must be contextualized within the broaderscale of polar operations: the cost of developing and maintaining a
digital twin represents a negligible fraction of outgoings compared
to the immense capital required for annual station maintenance,summer campaigns and vessel and/or aircraft logistics. Therefore,SDT funding should not be drawn from short-term, competitive
research grants; rather, its long-term viability must be anchored
directly within the core structural and operational maintenancebudgets of national polar programmes or research institutions.As recently highlighted by Morandini & Soutullo ( 2026), this shift
is critical: current funding mechanisms often prioritize project
novelty and visibility over institutional responsibility and long-
term continuity, a structural weakness that compromises thegovernance capacity of Antarctic science. By integrating SDTs intothese institutional budgets, akin to the sustained support provided
for physical infrastructure and field campaigns, these platforms
are elevated from temporary project lifecycles to permanentmanagement assets.
Ultimately, the effectiveness of a SDT depends less on tech-
nical ingenuity than on this sustained institutional commitment.
Beyond technical and financial hurdles, other systemic challenges
remain: the risk of algorithmic bias in automated decision-making(Sabriet al.2025), the socio-ethical implications of constant per-
sonnel monitoring for base dynamics (Mureddu et al.2025,C a n -
tisaniet al.
2026) and the administrative complexities of data
governance and institutional stewardship among national pro-grammes in the current shifting Antarctic landscape (Haward &Jackson 2023,D o d d s 2024a ,A r p i 2026). Thus, the transition to
a comprehensive SDT is not merely a technical upgrade but an
institutional evolution that requires strategic resource alignment.

operations, effectively bridging the gap between offshore activityand land-based research infrastructure. As these systems evolve,their implementation could progressively expand, enriching theirutility across multiple domains and serving a wide range of
stakeholders. Their ability to integrate real-time data, model infras-
tructure, monitor logistical operations and assess environmentalrisks positions SDTs as essential platforms for decision-making inremote regions.
Although this framework serves as a proof-of-concept for
an individual base, there is potential for it to evolve into aregional-scale system. The Antarctic Peninsula, characterizedby a high density of international programmes (COMNAP2017) and shared logistics, represents the logical next step for
such a development. Transitioning from a station-specific to
a regional model will require addressing significant technicaland geopolitical challenges, ranging from international datainteroperability to institutional frameworks under the Antarctic
Treaty System. Consequently, this work serves as a foundational
step: by establishing standardized protocols at a station level, weprovide the necessary building blocks for future collaborative,continent-wide digital infrastructure.
Beyond operational management, SDTs also enhance science
communication and outreach. As noted by the late Antarctic
researcher Andrés Barbosa, ‘The polar regions are very attractive,and we must take advantage of this circumstance to disseminatethem’ (García-Roa 2023). SDT models enable transparent and
accessible public communication, bringing Antarctic science
closer to the public while promoting education, outreach (Xavieret al. 2024,V i l aet al. 2025), international cooperation and
networking within the scientific community (Kim & Jung 2016,
Ríos-Silva et al. 2025). Much of the information that is available
for some research stations and refuges is currently documented

understanding of station activities. Although, as mentioned, the
full implementation of digital twins may face technological andlogistical limitations, the development of functional prototypesat selected research stations would represent a crucial first steptowards the digitalization of polar research infrastructure. Such
efforts could contribute to more coordinated, data-rich science,
which is crucial in light of the emerging conservation challengesin Antarctica that demand innovative tools and collaborativeapproaches from fragmented observation into coordinated fore-
